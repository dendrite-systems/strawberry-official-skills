# Plan a Marketing Campaign

Produce a campaign plan the team can approve and execute. Research what is needed to make the plan
credible; do not return a template or a list of questions and call the work complete.

## 1. Understand the campaign decision

Clarify the business outcome, audience, offer or product, market, timing, budget range, required
channels, constraints, and the decision the plan must support. Identify whether this is a launch,
always-on program, event, lifecycle campaign, experiment, or another campaign shape.

Use existing goals, brand guidance, audience work, prior campaigns, channel baselines, customer
evidence, and approved product material when available. Ask only about gaps that could materially
change the direction.

## 2. Ground the strategy in evidence

Use `strawberry/marketing/research-an-audience` when the audience is unclear or the evidence needs
proper synthesis. Use `strawberry/marketing/run-competitor-analysis` when competitor position or
activity could change the campaign. For a lighter gap, research it directly and preserve the
sources.

Distinguish current facts, team assumptions, platform estimates, prior results, and hypotheses.
Check that the proposed objective can plausibly connect to the business outcome and that the team
has, or can create, the required proof.

When the central proposition is genuinely unsettled, present a small number of distinct campaign
directions with their evidence and tradeoffs before building the full plan. Do not manufacture
options when one direction is already clear.

## 3. Build the campaign

Create a coherent plan that covers:

- the business outcome, campaign objective, and measurable success definition;
- the audience, situation, and evidence behind the choice;
- the proposition, message, proof, offer, and call to action;
- the role of each channel across discovery, consideration, conversion, or retention;
- content, asset, landing-page, operational, and technical needs;
- timing, sequence, owners, dependencies, approvals, and budget assumptions;
- measurement definitions, baseline, reporting cadence, and decision points; and
- risks, open questions, and what would cause the team to adjust or stop.

Make channel choices because they fit the audience, behavior, message, and goal—not because a
generic campaign template includes them. Name assumptions that would change the economics or
feasibility.

## 4. Validate the plan

Check that the audience, proposition, offer, channel roles, assets, timing, measurement, and budget
tell one consistent story. Look for unsupported claims, unclear ownership, missing dependencies,
conflicting dates, unmeasurable objectives, attribution gaps, and a plan that requires more content
or operations than the team can realistically deliver.

Deliver the plan in the team's useful format, with an executive summary, the decisions already
made, the decisions still needed, and source links beside consequential evidence. The result should
be ready for approval, not artificially polished past unresolved choices.

## 5. Stop at the campaign boundary

This skill stops before configuring platforms, launching a campaign, publishing content, contacting
people, or changing spend. Those are separate actions with their own current permissions and
review points.

After the plan is accepted, use `strawberry/marketing/build-a-content-plan` to turn its message and
channel needs into a content program, or
`strawberry/marketing/create-and-repurpose-marketing-content` when a specific approved asset is
ready to produce.

Follow Strawberry's active scoped permission for every source and action. Draft or ask when the
current permission is insufficient. Stop when identity, account, destination, scope, impact, or
sensitive-data handling changes, and verify completed external actions.

Preserve the accepted goals, assumptions, definitions, owners, and review points when the team wants
to reuse the method. Do not automate launch decisions merely because campaign preparation recurs.
